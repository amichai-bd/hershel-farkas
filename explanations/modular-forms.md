# Modular Forms

## Non-technical
Some functions on the upper half-plane are astonishingly symmetric: transforming the input by a whole infinite family of operations leaves them essentially unchanged. These modular forms appear all over number theory; their coefficients count things like ways to write numbers as sums of squares.

## Somewhat technical
$SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ acts on $\mathbb{H}$ by $\tau\mapsto\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}$. A modular form of weight $k$ for a subgroup $\Gamma$ is holomorphic $f$ with $f\big(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\big)=(c\tau+d)^k f(\tau)$, holomorphic at the cusps. Example: $\theta(0,\tau)^4$ is a weight-2 form for a congruence subgroup, and its coefficients count representations as sums of four squares.

## Engineers
The group $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is generated by two moves on $\tau$: $\tau\mapsto\tau+1$ and $\tau\mapsto-1/\tau$. Changing the basis of a lattice $\mathbb{Z}+\tau\mathbb{Z}$ changes $\tau$ by such moves without changing the lattice, so functions of the lattice's shape must be (nearly) invariant under them. A modular form is a function whose output transforms by a predictable scale factor $(c\tau+d)^k$, and whose Fourier ($q=e^{2\pi i\tau}$) coefficients are often integer counts, e.g. $\theta^4$ counts ways to write $n$ as a sum of four squares: a generating function you can compute and check numerically.

## Scientists
Modular invariance is the statement that physics on a torus cannot depend on which basis of its periods you choose; in string theory, one-loop amplitudes are integrals over the fundamental domain of $SL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ for exactly this reason, and higher-genus amplitudes use theta constants and the modular group $Sp(2g,\mathbb{Z})$ (D'Hoker–Phong, [arXiv:hep-th/0110247](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0110247)). Modular curves, quotients of the upper half-plane by subgroups $\Gamma(N)$, are the configuration spaces of such tori with extra level-$N$ structure.

## Mathematicians
The quotient $\Gamma(N)\backslash\mathbb{H}^*$ is a compact Riemann surface (modular curve). Farkas, Kra and Kopeliovich gave explicit maps of modular curves into low-dimensional projective spaces using theta constants, realizing their automorphism groups as restrictions of projective isometries and yielding generalizations of the Platonic solids (home page; "Uniformizations of Modular Curves," *Comm. Anal. Geom.* 1996; "Automorphic Forms for Subgroups of the Modular Group," *Israel J. Math.* 1993 and *J. Anal. Math.* 1996).
